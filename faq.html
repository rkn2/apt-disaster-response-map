What this map is

This is a free, public map of firms and individual professionals who work on historic buildings, marked to show which of them have documented experience relevant to disasters — either responding in the immediate aftermath, or doing the specialized work a historic building needs during the rebuild that follows.

It exists so that someone standing in front of a damaged historic building does not have to start from a blank search box. Each pin is a starting point for a phone call: a name, a location, what kind of work they do, and — where we could document it — specific past projects with links to the evidence.

It is meant for:

  • Owners and stewards of historic properties, before or after damage.
  • Emergency managers, preservation offices, and volunteer coordinators looking for qualified help in a specific area.
  • Anyone planning a historic rebuild once the emergency phase is over.

The map currently holds 343 entries — individual professionals and firms — and covers the United States, Canada, and a small number of other countries. It is a research project, not a service you can hire, and not a referral business. Nothing is sold here and no one pays to appear.

If you are listed here and would rather not be

You can ask to be taken off the map, and the request will be honored. You do not have to give a reason. See how to correct or remove a listing, or go straight to the update or remove a listing form.

Questions

Who is this map for?

Mostly for people who need to find a professional and do not already know the preservation field. After a fire, flood, hurricane, or earthquake, the hard part is often not deciding that a historic building is worth saving — it is finding someone who has actually done that kind of work before, and finding them quickly, nearby.

It is also useful before anything goes wrong: a property owner looking for someone to do a seismic retrofit, a flood-proofing study, or an emergency plan for a collection or a landmark building can use the same map.

Where do the listings come from?

Most entries come from the professional membership of the Association for Preservation Technology (APT): the College of Fellows roster, the list of APT Recognized Professionals, and APT's corporate, government, and nonprofit member organizations. A smaller number of entries — 14 of the 343 — were researched independently and have no APT affiliation at all. Those are drawn with a dashed outline on the map and labeled "independently researched" in their pop-up. They were held to the same evidence standard as everything else.

Membership rosters were used only to decide who to research. Everything else attached to an entry — the office location, the website, the contact information, the specialties, the disaster experience, the projects — was researched independently from public sources, principally each firm's or professional's own website. None of the contact information on this map was taken from an APT membership directory.

How were entries checked?

This is the part that took the most work, and it is the main thing that distinguishes this map from a directory listing. The rules were:

  • No claim without a source. Every specialty tag, every disaster tag, and every past project on an entry either carries a source URL in the research dataset or is explicitly recorded as unverified. A job title on its own earns nothing: "preservation engineer" is not evidence of disaster experience.
  • Claims were checked against the source cited for them. It is not enough that a source exists; the source has to actually say the thing.
  • A second, adversarial pass tried to break the entries. Cited pages were re-opened with the specific question "does this page really support this tag?" Tags whose evidence did not hold up on re-reading were removed, not left in place with a hopeful citation. Several disaster tags were deleted this way.
  • A source hierarchy. The entity's own website first; then reputable third parties such as news coverage, the National Park Service, state historic preservation offices, Parks Canada, the APT Bulletin, conference programs, awards, and universities. Business-directory aggregators were used only to confirm an address or phone number, never to justify a tag or a project.
  • Gaps were recorded, not filled in. "No public evidence found" is a legitimate result and appears on many entries. Nothing was guessed to make a record look fuller.

Behind the map, every entry also carries a verification status — verified (identity and location sourced, and every tag survived the adversarial check), partially verified (some tags were unverified or were removed), no public evidence (identity and location are sourced, the searches were done, and no disaster or specialty evidence exists publicly), or unverified (identity or location could not be confirmed). These statuses live in the underlying research dataset and are not currently shown in the map pop-ups.

What does "documented historic-preservation experience" actually require?

A disaster tag was only applied when the documented work was on a historic resource — a landmark, a listed or National Register-eligible property, or a building or site clearly treated as historic in the source. This is deliberate. Plenty of capable firms do post-fire or post-flood work on ordinary buildings; that work is real, but it is not the same as knowing how to dry out a nineteenth-century timber frame, stabilize a terracotta facade, or repoint historic masonry without destroying it. This map is trying to answer the narrower question, so general disaster-restoration experience alone does not earn a disaster tag here.

What do the colors mean? What are the two kinds of capability?

Entries are described along two separate axes, and the color of a pin is the combination of them:

  • Responder + rebuild specialist — documented acute disaster experience and hands-on restoration capability.
  • Acute disaster responder — documented disaster work, without a hands-on rebuild trade on file.
  • Rebuild-phase specialist — hands-on historic restoration capability, no documented disaster work on file.
  • General preservation trade — a preservation professional with neither a disaster tag nor a hands-on rebuild specialty recorded (for example, a planner, researcher, or documentation specialist).
Acute disaster response — the disaster tags
The work of the first days and weeks: rapid damage and safety assessment, emergency stabilization, mothballing, salvage, and the planning and mitigation work that happens before a disaster. The tags name the hazard or the activity — flood fire hurricane-wind earthquake-seismic tornado wildfire disaster-assessment hazard-mitigation, and others. These are the tags that had to clear the evidence bar described above, and only a minority of entries carry any.
Rebuild-phase relevance — the restoration trades
Whether an entry has the kind of hands-on capability a historic building needs once the rubble is cleared and the real repair starts: masonry, stone, terracotta, timber and windows, metals, roofing, plaster and stucco, conservation, restoration contracting, and the design and engineering that goes with them. This is a deliberately broad, inclusive flag, and it is derived from an entry's recorded specialties rather than researched separately — so most of the map qualifies. Read it as "this is the kind of work they do," not as a verified statement that they can handle your particular rebuild.

Because the two axes are independent, the map does not force a firm into a single category. It also means the two axes are not equally strict: the disaster tags are the carefully sourced ones.

Why does a materials supplier show up as rebuild-relevant with no disaster tags?

Because both statements are true at once. A manufacturer of replacement terracotta, or of historic lime mortar, has almost certainly never been dispatched to a disaster site — so it has no disaster tags, and it should not. But when a damaged landmark is being rebuilt, someone has to make the replacement units, and there are not many suppliers who can. That is a real part of the recovery, so these entries are marked rebuild-relevant and left honestly empty on the disaster axis.

An entry has no disaster tags. Does that mean they have no disaster experience?

No. It means no public documentation of that experience was found within the research done for this project. Small firms and individual craftspeople often have decades of relevant work and no website describing it; some professionals work as subcontractors and are never named publicly on the projects they worked on. Empty tags are an honest record of what could be documented, not a judgment about capability. If an entry looks close to what you need, call and ask.

Is everyone qualified in my area on this map?

Almost certainly not. Coverage is uneven, and it reflects where APT's membership is concentrated rather than where disasters happen. Some states with high disaster exposure have only one entry or none, while parts of the Northeast and Midwest are dense. An empty area on the map means this project has no entry there — not that no qualified professional exists there. Local preservation offices, your state historic preservation office, and regional trade organizations are good next stops.

What personal information is shown, and where do the pins actually point?

Only firm-level, publicly posted contact information appears: a main office phone number, a general inquiry inbox such as an "info@" address, or a contact-page link. There are no personal email addresses, no direct lines, no cell numbers, and no home addresses on this map — including in cases where such details were publicly findable.

Individual professionals are placed at their professional location: the office they work out of, or otherwise the city given in a public professional listing. A pin over a person's name is a work location, never a residence. For sole practitioners whose business address may also be a home address, we place the pin only at the level publicly presented as the business location; if that is still more precise than you are comfortable with, tell us and it will be changed or removed.

Does appearing on this map mean someone is endorsed or qualified?

No. Inclusion is not an endorsement, a certification, a license, a rating, or a recommendation. This project does not evaluate the quality of anyone's work, does not check licensure or insurance, does not interview references, and does not resolve disputes. What was checked is narrower and specific: that a described claim traces to a public source, and that the source supports it.

Deciding whether a particular professional is right for your building is your responsibility. Before engaging anyone, verify their current credentials, license, insurance, and references directly with them and with the relevant licensing authority, and satisfy yourself that they are a fit for your project. See the disclaimer below.

How current is this?

The research was carried out in 2026, and each record notes when it was last checked. Information ages: people change firms, retire, or close their practices; phone numbers and websites change; capability and availability change. A listing being present does not mean the person is currently practicing, currently available, or currently taking work of the kind you need. Confirm anything important directly with the professional.

How do I get added, or get an entry fixed?

If you are a preservation professional or firm with documented disaster-response or historic restoration work and you are not on the map, you can propose a listing on the suggest a listing page. Suggestions are researched against public sources before anything is added, using the same standards described above — so it helps a lot if you can point to public pages that show the work.

If something about an existing entry is wrong — a stale phone number, an old employer, a project misattributed, a specialty listed that you do not actually offer — use the update or remove a listing page. Corrections are welcome and do not need to be justified.

How do I get removed?

Use the update or remove a listing page and ask to be removed. You do not need to explain why, and you do not need anyone else's permission to ask about your own listing.

Removal requests are honored. The entry is taken off the public map when the map is next regenerated, and the person or firm is not re-added by a later research pass. Nothing on this map is here because someone opted in, so no one should have to argue to get out of it.

If that page does not work for you for any reason, email nap@psu.edu directly and say you want your listing removed. That is enough — no form and no explanation required.

If you are surprised to find yourself here: this map was built from public professional rosters and public websites, it lists no personal contact details, and it is a university research project rather than a commercial directory. If you would like it corrected instead of deleted — for example, kept but with different specialties, or with a different office location — that is equally welcome.

Who made this, and who pays for it?

The map is maintained by APT's Disaster Response Initiative and the Heritage3D Lab in the Department of Architectural Engineering at Penn State, led by Rebecca Napolitano. It is free to use and free to be listed on. It is not a commercial service, it does not accept payment for placement, it does not sell or share the data it displays, and it earns nothing from the connections it helps people make.

The map itself is a single static web page using Leaflet and OpenStreetMap tiles. It has no login and no tracking of who looks at what.

Listings are descriptive, not qualifying.

Verify before you engage anyone. It is the reader's responsibility to independently confirm a professional's current credentials, licenses, insurance, references, and fitness for the specific work before hiring or relying on them — and to comply with any permitting, review, or procurement requirements that apply to a historic property.

Information may be incomplete, outdated, or wrong. Entries are compiled from public sources at a point in time. Firms merge, dissolve, and relocate; people change employers and retire; websites and phone numbers go stale; capability and availability change. Absence of a tag does not mean absence of experience, and the map is not a comprehensive census of qualified professionals — geographic coverage is uneven, and many capable people and firms are not on it. Nothing here should be treated as a complete list of the options available to you.

This map does not provide engineering, architectural, conservation, legal, insurance, or safety advice, and it must not be used to judge whether a damaged structure is safe to enter or occupy.
